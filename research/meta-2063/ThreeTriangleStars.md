# Three noncollinear point-stars require fourteen replacement gates

Choose three noncollinear points of the projective plane of order three. A monotone binary circuit that accepts their twelve incident point–line input pairs and rejects every quadrangle test needs **fourteen gates**, even when every OR of any subset of the lines through each of the ten outside points is free. Three ordinary stars attain fourteen. Together with the collinear case, this establishes the exact local cost for every choice of three centers. A circuit reorganizing four or more stars or using other shared signals is still possible under the project's broader question. [Collinear case](ThreeCollinearStars.md) · [Project statement](https://pequalnp-desk.pequalnp.workers.dev/desk/row?gap=L-meta-2063)

## Restrict the available signals

The centers form a triangle. There are three side lines, each shared by two centers, and six private lines, two through each center. On the twelve positives all other raw variables are zero. Removing irrelevant line variables from a cached OR keeps its positive values and can only lower its negative values. The resulting pool contains twelve raw variables and twenty-two additional signals. Complete stars at outside centers are zero on the twelve positives.

Suppose a replacement used at most thirteen gates. The connected subcircuit supplying its output can refer to at most fourteen distinct sources. For each positive and negative, its source set must contain a signal true on the positive and false on the negative. The verifier enumerates solutions of these finite hitting conditions. If any raw source is omitted, exactly fourteen sources are necessary. Taking all twelve raw signals with any two others, and adding the feasible fourteen-source menus which omit a raw signal, gives 243 menus in total. Every smaller feasible set consists of all raw signals and at most one extra. A circuit using fourteen sources and thirteen gates must be a read-once formula, by counting its outgoing wire occurrences.

Retain the centers, the three side lines, and one private line per center. Set all other private lines to zero. Four choices of representatives induce the same nine positive rows and twenty-one maximal negative rows on these nine raw coordinates. Required positives using retained lines still hold; reducing a negative row cannot turn it true.

Deleting $d$ distinct reachable sources by setting them to zero saves at least $d$ gates whenever the simplified output remains nonconstant. Indeed, in a connected output circuit with $g$ binary gates and $s$ sources, the underlying connected undirected multigraph has $2g$ edges, $s+g$ vertices and cycle rank $g-s+1$. Simplifying an OR with a zero operand contracts its other input edge; an AND with a zero operand is discarded and its zero is propagated. Pruning dead wires and gates only deletes edges or vertices. These operations cannot increase cycle rank. The final graph has at most $s-d$ sources, so it has at most $g-d$ gates. If two surviving sources have identical values after restriction, this count can be made before identifying them.

For each individual extra signal, one can choose the representatives so it collapses to zero or a raw variable. Thus an original circuit on all raw inputs plus at most one extra reduces to at most ten gates on the nine raw coordinates, since three private source variables disappear. The same reasoning covers 210 of the fourteen-source menus. For the remaining 33, the original circuit was read-once. Under a suitable restriction at least three of its source leaves vanish and just one surviving signal combines two or more raw variables. The verifier excludes all read-once formulas over these specific surviving leaves, allowing any subset of them to be used. Both the finite source and formula checks are included below.

## The nine-input problem

All nine raw variables are essential. On singleton inputs the output is false, and on every two-variable input it is true exactly for the nine specified incidences. This follows by testing which pairs lie below a negative row. Their graph is connected and no two vertices have the same set of neighbors.

Consider a proper nonempty set $S$ of raw variables which enters a circuit only through one intermediate signal $h$ depending solely on $S$. Since the pair graph is connected, an edge crosses from $S$ to its complement. To distinguish that pair from its outside endpoint alone, $h$ must be true at the inside endpoint alone. With all outside variables zero, the circuit therefore rejects $h=1$, since it rejects that singleton. It also rejects $h=0$ there. This forbids an accepted pair internal to $S$. Consequently every vertex in $S$ has an outside neighbor, and $h$ must be true at each such singleton. Each vertex of $S$ then has exactly the same outside neighbors: the rest of the circuit receives the same inputs when one such singleton is swapped for another. The graph has no such twins, so $S$ has just one vertex.

Ten gates on nine essential sources permit at most two outgoing wire occurrences beyond the one required at each reachable nonoutput vertex. This gives a short exhaustive description of every possible circuit.

- If only raw variables are repeated, the output is a read-once formula on the nine raw inputs with up to two extra raw occurrences. The repeats can be the same variable.
- Otherwise a shared internal signal is required. Without a repeated raw input, the earliest shared internal gate roots a private tree. Its raw leaves could enter the rest of the circuit only through that gate, contradicting the preceding argument. If it contains every raw variable, the entire circuit beyond it receives just one signal and simplifies away.
- The remaining possibility uses both extra occurrences. One raw variable $r$ is used twice and one internal signal $h$ is used twice. Every other signal is used once. A minimal circuit must use $r$ once inside the private formula for $h$ and once outside it. Using $r$ zero or two times inside would again make the entire set of leaves feeding $h$ private, contrary to the same argument. A gate computing a function of only one raw variable can be bypassed.

Enumerate $r$ and the other raw inputs used only inside $h$. The signal $h$ is a read-once formula on those inputs and one occurrence of $r$. The remainder is a read-once formula on the outside raw variables, the second occurrence of $r$ and two occurrences of $h$. If the outside raw variables fail to distinguish a positive row from a negative row, then $h$ must be true on that positive and false on that negative. Only fifteen candidate shared traces survive these tests after removal of traces with identical positive values and more negative values. None admits a suitable outer formula.

The read-once tests recursively partition available leaf occurrences. At any binary root the smaller child uses at most half the available leaves. An OR child must reject every required zero; positives it accepts are removed from the sibling's obligations. An AND child must accept every required one; negatives it rejects are removed from the sibling's obligations. Raw leaves, both root operations and all eligible subsets are tested. This excludes the nine-input, ten-gate circuit and the 33 additional menus. An independent exact ten-gate SAT formulation also returned unsatisfiable on the nine-input core.
